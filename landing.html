🔬 Architecture

How GoldBot was built

We identified critical failure modes in generic AI financial chatbots and designed GoldBot to solve them from the ground up.

1
LangGraph Parallel Subgraphs
3 category subgraphs — Macro, Safe Haven, Geopolitical — fetch all 9 factors in parallel before feeding into the scoring node.
2
RAG-Grounded Scoring
A FAISS vector store retrieves each factor's historical causal relationship to gold, giving the LLM real evidence rather than relying purely on its training data.
3
Deterministic Weighting
The LLM scores each factor -10 to +10 qualitatively. Python applies locked domain-expert weights (summing to 100) to calculate Strength % mathematically.
4
Real-time API Integration
Live data from FRED API (yields, rates, inflation), yfinance (USD, VIX, S&P 500), and Tavily MCP (central bank buying, geopolitical risk).

GoldBot: A Multi-Factor Safe-Haven Analyzer for Gold — Cross-Regime Evidence for Trend-Matching Rather Than Event Discrimination

Raj Tejpal Khatik · MSc Applied AI, University of Warwick
Live preprint on SSRN · Currently under review at a peer-reviewed journal
📄 Read Live on SSRN ↗ Live Demo ↗
Abstract
Gold's role as a safe-haven asset is one of the most robust stylized facts in empirical finance (Baur & McDermott, 2010; Baur & Lucey, 2010), yet systems built to operationalize this behavior in real time are rarely subjected to rigorous, cross-regime backtesting. We present GoldBot, a nine-factor, LLM-orchestrated system that classifies gold's safe-haven status (ON/OFF) and quantifies its Strength% via a transparent weighted-scoring architecture with retrieval-augmented context. We backtest GoldBot across four independently constructed samples spanning four structurally distinct macro-financial regimes: a stratified sample of 60 dates during a 2025-26 gold uptrend (Backtest 1), a set of 18 genuine macro/geopolitical events spanning both an uptrend and a subsequent downtrend within the same window (Backtest 2), 20 stratified dates during the 2020 COVID crash and recovery (Backtest 3), and 20 stratified dates during the 2008 global financial crisis (Backtest 4).
Using McNemar's exact test throughout, we find GoldBot underperforms a naive always-safe-haven-ON baseline in three of four regimes — significantly so in the largest sample (Backtest 1: 35.0% vs. 66.7%, n=60, p=0.018) — and ties it exactly in the fourth (Backtest 3: 90.0% vs. 90.0%, n=20). Critically, decomposition reveals this is not evidence of skill: in every regime tested, GoldBot's apparent accuracy is fully explained by whether its prevailing directional bias in that period happens to align with the ambient price trend, not by discrimination between distinct real-world events.
Across 18 genuinely distinct macro/geopolitical events in Backtest 2 — spanning a war outbreak, a war-ending ceasefire, a historic 54-45 Fed chair confirmation vote, and a shock OPEC exit — GoldBot's Strength% output never left a 37–50% range (SD=4.2 points), indicating near-total insensitivity to event content within a normal-range macro regime. By contrast, in the 2020 and 2008 regimes, where underlying quantitative factors reached genuinely extreme values (negative real yields, VIX above 60), GoldBot's output did shift meaningfully, suggesting the model's quantitative factors retain some responsiveness to extreme readings even as its qualitative/geopolitical scoring remains substantially compressed. We argue this cross-regime evidence for trend-matching, rather than event discrimination, is a more precise and more damaging diagnosis than any single backtest could provide, and a necessary standard for evaluating this class of system.

1. Introduction and Problem Statement
Gold's function as a hedge and safe haven against equity market stress is among the most extensively documented regularities in international finance. Baur and McDermott (2010) provide cross-country evidence that gold acts as both hedge and safe haven for major developed markets, while Baur and Lucey (2010) formalize the weak/strong distinction between hedge and safe-haven behavior. Systems that attempt to operationalize this behavior as a live, LLM-scored classification — as we do with GoldBot — face a specific validation challenge: a single backtest, run within a single macro-financial regime, cannot distinguish genuine event discrimination from a lucky (or unlucky) alignment between a system's incidental output bias and the ambient trend of that regime.
We address this directly by backtesting GoldBot across four structurally distinct regimes: two windows within its live 2025-26 deployment period (an uptrend and a subsequent downtrend), the 2020 COVID crash and recovery, and the 2008 global financial crisis. This design allows us to ask a sharper question than "how accurate is GoldBot" — namely, "does GoldBot's accuracy, wherever observed, reflect genuine discrimination between distinct events, or merely coincidental alignment with whatever trend happens to prevail in the sampled window." We find decisive evidence for the latter.
2. Related Work
Gold as hedge and safe haven. Baur and McDermott (2010) test gold's status against equities across a 30-year, multi-country sample, finding it functions as both hedge and safe haven for major developed markets. Baur and Lucey (2010) introduce the weak/strong safe-haven distinction, which our regime-classification ground truth (gold/equity MA50 divergence) operationalizes as a point-in-time check.
Single-factor channels. Joy (2011) documents gold's shifting relationship with the US dollar over time, motivating GoldBot's inclusion of the USD Index as a distinct factor.
Systematic geopolitical risk measurement. Caldara and Iacoviello (2022) construct the GPR index from newspaper text; we validate GoldBot's geopolitical_risk scoring directly against this index in Section 5.6.
Gap addressed. No existing work, to our knowledge, subjects an LLM-orchestrated safe-haven classification system to backtesting across multiple structurally distinct historical macro-financial regimes specifically to test for trend-matching versus genuine event discrimination. This cross-regime design is this paper's primary methodological contribution.

3.2 Scoring and aggregation
Each factor is scored by an LLM (GPT-4o-mini, temperature=0) on a bounded -10 to +10 scale. The weighted sum normalizes to a 0-100 Strength% score; Safe Haven status is ON if Strength% >= 50.
4. System Architecture
GoldBot is implemented as a LangGraph state machine with three parallel fetch subgraphs (macro, safe-haven-specific, geopolitical) converging on a generate_prediction node. Quantitative factors are fetched from FRED/yfinance; qualitative factors (central bank gold buying, geopolitical risk) are retrieved via live Tavily web search. A hard override forces any factor to neutral when its data fetch fails.

5.1 Methodology overview
Four backtests, each constructed independently:

1Stratified random dates60 (20+20+20, three independent seeds)Sept 2025–June 2026Uptrend

Ground truth throughout: gold's close relative to its own 50-day moving average (price direction); gold-vs-S&P500 MA divergence (regime classification, Baur & Lucey's weak/strong distinction operationalized point-in-time). Backtests 3 and 4 use forced-neutral Tier 2 factors throughout (no historical text recovery attempted, consistent with the same principle applied elsewhere, and additionally necessary since GDELT's coverage does not reliably extend to 2008). Statistical treatment: McNemar's exact test throughout for paired comparisons against the naive baseline, given both methods are scored on identical dates within each backtest.
5.2 Backtest 1: stratified sample, expanded to n=60
Backtest 1 was constructed incrementally: an initial n=20 sample, followed by two independent expansion batches of n=20 each (distinct random seeds, non-overlapping dates), reaching n=60.

This is the paper's only individually statistically significant result and reflects a stable, well-powered finding: during this uptrend window, GoldBot's systematic bias toward OFF classifications actively works against a naive trend-following rule.
5.3 Backtest 2: genuine events, expanded to n=18, and the output-compression finding
The original 10-date sample (7 FOMC meetings, a VIX spike, the Feb 28, 2026 Iran war outbreak, and the first Warsh-chaired FOMC meeting) was expanded with 8 additional independently-verified genuine events: Warsh's formal nomination (Mar 4), the initial US-Iran ceasefire (Apr 8), Warsh's confirmation hearing and the ceasefire's indefinite extension (Apr 21), the UAE's shock OPEC exit (Apr 28), Warsh's confirmation as Fed Chair in the most divisive vote in Fed history (May 13), the Powell-Warsh transition (May 15), the Iran war memorandum of understanding (Jun 14), and the Lake Lucerne Hormuz negotiations summit (Jun 21).
ConfigurationGoldBot accuracyNaive baseline
Original n=10 (forced-neutral)40.0%70.0%
Original n=10 (GDELT-restored)30.0%70.0%
New 8 dates (GDELT-integrated)100.0%12.5%

The naive baseline of 12.5% on the 8 new dates reflects that gold traded below its own 50-day moving average on 7 of these 8 dates, meaning the always-ON rule was correct on only 1 of 8 (the March 4 Warsh nomination date). The combined n=18 naive baseline of 44.4% (8/18) is the weighted average of the original 10-date baseline (7/10 correct) and this 8-date subset (1/8 correct).
The reversal from n=10 to n=18 is not evidence of improved skill. The 8 new dates happen to fall within a subsequent gold downtrend (every one of the 8 shows gold trading below its own MA50), and GoldBot's near-constant tendency toward OFF classifications — the same bias that hurt it during the uptrend-dominated original 10 dates — mechanically aligns with a downtrend. The naive always-ON baseline, conversely, collapses from 70% (original 10 dates) to just 12.5% on these 8 new dates for the identical reason, pulling the combined baseline down to 44.4%.
The more diagnostic finding is output compression. Across all 18 genuinely distinct events — including a war outbreak, a war-ending ceasefire signing, a historic Fed confirmation vote, and a shock OPEC exit — GoldBot's Strength% output never left a 37–50% range (SD=4.2 percentage points). The model does not appear to meaningfully distinguish between these substantively very different real-world developments.
Case study, Feb 28, 2026 (Iran war outbreak): Full factor decomposition (weighted contributions) yields a Strength% of exactly 49% (verified: (-15+1000)/2000x100 = 49.25 -> 49%), one point below the ON threshold. The LLM assigned geopolitical_risk a score of +5 out of a possible +10 — correctly directional, but using only half its available range for what the input text described as an assassination-triggered war. A counterfactual recomputation shows that using the existing scale's maximum (+10, no architectural change required) would have been sufficient to cross the threshold (Strength% = 52%, ON).

This is not evidence of genuine crisis-detection: the underlying quantitative factors reached values never seen in GoldBot's other test windows — real yields went negative (-0.02% to -0.65%, vs. 1.7–2.3% in the 2025-26 window), the Fed funds rate collapsed to near-zero (0.05–0.65%), and VIX spiked as high as 64.0 (vs. a ceiling of ~30 elsewhere). These extreme quantitative readings mechanically pushed GoldBot's weighted-sum output to a constant ON — which then happened to match this period's strong gold uptrend (18 of 20 dates above MA50), producing an exact tie with the naive baseline rather than any demonstrated edge.

Unlike Backtests 2 and 3, GoldBot's output was not constant here — real yields swung from 1.44% to 3.14%, and VIX ranged from 21 to a peak of 69.2, producing genuine variation in the model's classifications (16 ON, 4 OFF). Despite this variability, GoldBot still underperformed the naive baseline (60% vs. 70%), though the difference is not statistically significant at this sample size (p=0.625). This is directionally consistent with every other regime tested except the COVID tie.
5.6 Validation against the Caldara-Iacoviello GPR Index
GoldBot's geopolitical_risk scores across the original 10 Backtest 2 events were compared against the published GPR index at both monthly and daily resolution. Monthly: Pearson r=0.577 (p=0.081). Daily: Pearson r=0.586 (p=0.075). Both indicate a moderate, directionally positive, but not statistically significant correlation at n=10. The February 28 war outbreak's own day registers a daily GPR reading of 89.5 — lower than several preceding "quiet" days — while March 1–2 registers the window's highest reading (500.8), revealing a one-to-three-day event-to-coverage lag in the GPR methodology itself for rapid weekend-onset events, rather than a resolution artifact of our comparison.

Across four independently constructed backtests spanning four structurally distinct macro-financial regimes, GoldBot has never once demonstrated accuracy exceeding what a trend-following naive rule achieves in a way attributable to genuine skill. Where it appears to "win" (the 8-date downtrend expansion, where the naive baseline collapses to just 12.5%) or "tie" (2020), this is fully explained by its incidental directional bias in that period happening to align with the ambient trend — not by discrimination between the substantively different real-world events each test contains. Where its output does vary meaningfully (2008), it still underperforms the naive rule. We consider this the strongest and most complete evidentiary base in the paper, precisely because it required no single test to carry the full weight of the conclusion.
6. Generalization (Cross-Regime)
GoldBot's architecture generalizes mechanically across all four regimes tested — the pipeline runs, fetches data, and produces a classification regardless of the historical period. But mechanical generalization does not imply generalization of genuine predictive skill: the cross-regime evidence in Section 5.7 shows accuracy is regime-contingent through trend-matching alone, not through any demonstrated capacity to discriminate between distinct real-world events within a regime.
7. Limitations
1. Backtests 3 and 4 use forced-neutral Tier 2 factors throughout, with no GDELT-based historical recovery attempted (2008 predates reliable GDELT coverage entirely; this was extended to 2020 for consistency across the two historical regimes rather than selectively).

2. Backtest 4's naive-baseline comparison is not individually statistically significant (p=0.625) at n=20, though directionally consistent with the significant Backtest 1 finding and the non-significant Backtest 2 pattern.

3. The GPR validation (Section 5.6) uses only the original 10 Backtest 2 dates, not the expanded 18; extending this validation to the new 8 events is a natural next step.

4. Regime-divergence ground truth excludes co-directional markets in all four backtests, reducing effective sample size for the regime-classification metric specifically.

5. No formal multiple-comparisons correction was applied across the several significance tests reported in this paper; given that most tests returned non-significant or only marginally significant results, such a correction would not alter the paper's conclusions.

6. Backtest 2's 8-date expansion, while comprising genuinely verified real events, was not selected via a pre-registered, fully systematic search protocol — we searched for and verified additional events until reaching a defensible set, stopping short of a forced round number (10) when further candidates (e.g., Bank of Japan decisions) did not meet the bar for genuine market "shock" events.
8. Conclusion and Future Work
We present GoldBot, a nine-factor safe-haven analyzer for gold, and subject it to backtesting across four independently constructed samples spanning four structurally distinct macro-financial regimes — a novel design choice motivated by the recognition that any single-regime backtest cannot distinguish genuine event discrimination from coincidental trend alignment. Our central finding is unambiguous: across all four regimes, GoldBot's accuracy is fully explained by whether its incidental directional bias happens to match the ambient price trend, with the one exact tie (2020) explained by extreme quantitative factor readings mechanically flipping that bias into trend-alignment, and the one statistically significant result (Backtest 1, n=60, p=0.018) demonstrating a genuine, confirmed underperformance against a trivial baseline. The output-compression finding (Section 5.3) further shows that even within a single regime, GoldBot's response to genuinely severe qualitative events (a war outbreak, a historic Fed confirmation) is muted to the point of near-insensitivity, while its response to extreme quantitative readings (2020's negative real yields, 2008's VIX spikes) is more substantial.
Future work should prioritize: (a) prompt-level recalibration anchoring the +/-10 geopolitical scoring scale to concrete historical reference events, validated via the counterfactual methodology demonstrated in Section 5.3; (b) extending the GPR validation to the full 18-event Backtest 2 sample; (c) reassessing the 50% classification threshold's calibration specifically under trending versus range-bound conditions; (d) replication in a genuine bear-market regime for gold specifically (distinct from the equity bear markets sampled in Backtests 3 and 4) to complete the cross-regime evidentiary base.
