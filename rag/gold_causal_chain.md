# Gold Price Causal Chain: Factor-by-Factor Historical Relationships

## Real Yields (TIPS)
Real yields represent the inflation-adjusted return on holding US Treasury securities. Gold pays no yield or dividend, so it competes directly with real yields as a store of value. When real yields rise, the opportunity cost of holding gold increases, since investors forgo a growing risk-free return by holding a non-yielding asset instead. Historically, gold has shown a strong inverse relationship with real yields: during 2003-2011, as real yields fell and turned negative, gold rallied from around $300 to over $1,900 per ounce. Conversely, when real yields rose sharply in 2013 (the "taper tantrum"), gold fell over 25% that year. This is considered gold's single most reliable macro driver by most institutional analysts.

For scoring purposes: real yields below 0% (negative) are strongly bullish for gold, as seen in 2020-2021 when gold hit record highs. Real yields between 0-1% are mildly bullish. Real yields between 1-2% are neutral to mildly bearish. Real yields above 2% are strongly bearish, representing a high opportunity cost environment historically associated with gold underperformance, as seen during 2022-2023.

## USD Index (DXY)
Gold is priced and traded globally in US dollars, so dollar strength directly affects gold's price for non-dollar buyers. When the dollar strengthens against a basket of major currencies, gold becomes more expensive for buyers using euros, yen, rupees, or other currencies, which typically reduces demand and pressures the price down. This relationship is near-mechanical and one of the most consistent in commodity markets, though it can be temporarily overridden by strong safe-haven demand during acute crises.

For scoring purposes: DXY below 95 indicates a weak dollar and is bullish for gold. DXY between 95-102 is within a normal historical range and should score neutral to mildly bearish depending on trend direction. DXY above 105 indicates dollar strength and is bearish for gold; above 110 is strongly bearish, representing multi-year dollar strength last seen during 2022's aggressive Fed tightening cycle.

## Fed Rate / FOMC Policy
Federal Reserve interest rate decisions and forward guidance are the primary trigger mechanism that transmits into gold's price through two channels: real yields and the US dollar. A hawkish Fed (signaling higher rates or faster tightening) tends to push both real yields and the dollar higher simultaneously, creating a compounding bearish pressure on gold. A dovish pivot has the opposite compounding effect. Because Fed communications are the root cause behind real yield and dollar movements, sharp, unexpected hawkish or dovish surprises from FOMC meetings or Fed officials' speeches often produce the most volatile single-day gold price reactions.

For scoring purposes, score based on policy direction and level combined: a rate above 5% or in an active hiking cycle is strongly bearish for gold. A rate in the 3-5% range that is stable or in a pausing phase is mildly bearish to neutral. A rate below 3% or in an active cutting cycle is bullish, as lower rates reduce the opportunity cost of holding gold and typically accompany dollar weakness.

## Central Bank Gold Buying
Since 2022, central bank gold purchasing has emerged as a genuinely new structural demand driver, distinct from the cyclical, sentiment-driven flows that dominated gold markets in prior decades. Following Russia's foreign currency reserves being frozen by Western sanctions after its invasion of Ukraine, a number of central banks — most notably China, India, Turkey, Poland, and Russia itself — significantly increased gold purchases as part of a broader strategy to reduce reliance on US dollar-denominated reserve assets. According to World Gold Council survey data, central banks have purchased over 1,000 tonnes of gold annually in recent years, roughly double the pace seen in the decade prior to 2022. This buying is considered structurally persistent rather than tactical, since central banks typically hold multi-decade investment horizons and are far less sensitive to short-term price swings than private investors.

For scoring purposes: this factor should be scored based on the described trend intensity rather than a single number. Reports of record or accelerating buying (above the ~1,000 tonne/year pace established since 2022) should score strongly bullish. Reports of buying continuing at a stable pace should score moderately bullish. Reports of slowing or declining central bank purchases should score neutral to mildly bullish, since this remains a structurally supportive factor even when decelerating.

## 2-Year Treasury Yield
The 2-year Treasury yield is the market's most direct, fast-reacting gauge of near-term Federal Reserve policy expectations, since it reflects what investors expect the average interest rate to be over a short horizon dominated by Fed decisions rather than long-run growth or inflation assumptions. It typically moves within minutes to hours of a Fed announcement or surprising economic data release, making it a useful high-frequency signal for detecting the immediate market reaction to policy shocks. Because it is a nominal (not inflation-adjusted) yield, it can diverge from real yields when inflation expectations are simultaneously shifting, offering a way to distinguish between "rates went up because of hawkish policy" versus "rates went up because inflation expectations rose."

For scoring purposes: a 2-year yield below 3% suggests markets expect significant Fed easing and is bullish for gold. A yield between 3-4.5% is within a normal recent range and should score neutral to mildly bearish. A yield above 4.5% suggests markets expect the Fed to hold rates high or hike further, which is bearish for gold, consistent with elevated opportunity cost.

## VIX (CBOE Volatility Index)
The VIX measures the market's expectation of near-term S&P 500 volatility, derived from options pricing, and is widely used as a proxy for investor fear or complacency. Gold has historically benefited from VIX spikes, as nervous investors rotate into traditional safe-haven assets during periods of elevated uncertainty. This relationship was clearly visible during the 2008 financial crisis, the 2020 COVID crash, and various geopolitical shock events, where sharp VIX spikes coincided with gold demand surges, though gold's reaction is not always immediate, sometimes lagging by a few trading days as investors first move to cash before reallocating to gold.

For scoring purposes, VIX should be judged against its historical range, not treated as automatically bullish whenever it is non-zero. As a rough guide: VIX below 15 indicates a calm, complacent market and should score neutral to mildly bearish for gold's safe-haven case, since low volatility typically coincides with risk-on conditions favoring equities over defensive assets. VIX in the 15-20 range is close to its long-term historical average (roughly 19-20) and represents normal market conditions, warranting a neutral score unless other factors suggest otherwise. VIX in the 20-25 range indicates elevated concern and should score mildly to moderately bullish for gold. VIX above 25 indicates significant market stress and should score strongly bullish for gold's safe-haven activation, consistent with historical crisis episodes. A VIX reading should always be considered relative to its own recent 52-week range where possible, since what counts as "elevated" can shift over multi-year periods of structurally higher or lower volatility regimes.

## Geopolitical Conflict / War Risk
Acute geopolitical events — armed conflicts, terrorist attacks, sanctions escalations, or sudden diplomatic breakdowns — tend to produce sharp, event-driven spikes in gold demand as investors seek an asset perceived as insulated from any single government's monetary or fiscal policy. Unlike central bank buying, which is a slow-moving structural trend, geopolitical risk premiums in gold tend to be short-lived, often fading within weeks once the immediate uncertainty resolves or markets adapt to a "new normal." Analysts increasingly note, however, that gold's role has evolved from a purely reactionary crisis hedge toward more of a persistent strategic reserve holding, particularly as central bank buying has layered a structural floor beneath what was previously a more purely event-driven demand source.

For scoring purposes: this factor should be scored based on the severity and immediacy described in the retrieved content rather than a numerical scale. Active, escalating armed conflict or major sanctions events should score strongly bullish. Ongoing but stable tensions without escalation should score mildly bullish. Diplomatic de-escalation or resolution of prior tensions should score neutral to mildly bearish.

## S&P 500 1-Month Growth %
Equity market performance and gold often exhibit an inverse rotation relationship during periods of market stress: when stocks are rising steadily, capital tends to flow toward equities and away from defensive assets like gold, while sharp equity selloffs typically trigger a flight-to-safety rotation that benefits gold. This relationship is not always consistent during calm, steadily rising markets, where gold and stocks can rise together (for instance, during broad-based liquidity expansion), but it becomes notably more reliable during genuine risk-off episodes, sharp corrections, or crisis periods, where the inverse relationship strengthens considerably.

For scoring purposes: a 1-month decline of more than 5% indicates a genuine risk-off event and should score strongly bullish for gold. A decline of 0-5% is a mild risk-off signal and should score mildly bullish. Growth of 0-3% represents normal market conditions and should score neutral. Growth above 3% in a month indicates strong risk-on sentiment and should score mildly to moderately bearish for gold, reflecting the rotation away from defensive assets.

## CPI / Inflation Expectations (5-Year Breakeven)
Gold's reputation as an "inflation hedge" is one of the most widely cited but empirically inconsistent relationships in commodity markets. While gold has preserved purchasing power over very long time horizons (multiple decades), its short-term correlation with realized or expected inflation is notably weaker and less reliable than popular narrative suggests — gold underperformed during several periods of high realized inflation (such as parts of the 1980s-1990s) and has sometimes risen during periods of low inflation expectations, driven instead by real yields, dollar weakness, or safe-haven flows. The 5-year breakeven inflation rate, derived from the spread between nominal and TIPS yields, is a more theoretically forward-looking measure than backward-looking CPI data, but even this forward measure shows a weaker, less consistent relationship with gold than real yields or the dollar index.
